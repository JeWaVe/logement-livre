Part One — A theory of rent and space · Chapter 1

1 Land does not produce, it conditions

Capitalist production therefore develops the technique and the combination of the social process of production only by simultaneously exhausting the two sources from which all wealth springs: the land and the worker.

Karl Marx, Capital, Book I, chap. xv (Marx 1867).

The life of a society rests on many things, of which land is the most anciently disputed and the most constantly misunderstood. A long tradition makes of it a productive power: even today, many regard the owner as someone who “makes available” a useful good, as one renders a service. This is, moreover, inscribed as such in the national accounts, whose gross domestic product (GDP) includes actual and imputed rents. This chapter argues the contrary, and lays it down at the outset — before any analysis of capitalism — as the thesis on which the whole book depends: land does not produce, it conditions. It takes part in no process of production; it is its condition. Rent is the name of the toll collected by whoever holds that condition. The definition is deliberately prior to Marx: it presupposes neither the market, nor wage labour, nor the law of value, and holds for every sedentary society — from the temple of Uruk to today’s landlord.

1.1 Land produces no value

The objection comes at once, and it must be given its full force: wheat does grow out of the earth, not out of the pavement. The land feeds, bears, shelters, yields its mineral content, and nothing is done without it: this is granted without reserve. The land is, in Marx’s phrase, an “agent in the production of a use-value”: the product exists, material, and the land has something to do with it. But Marx immediately adds the distinction that decides everything: the land “intervenes in no way in the formation of value”.1 To produce a use-value (to make a useful thing exist) is not to add wealth in the sense economics gives the word: what works is the ploughing, the sowing, the harvest, with their tools; the land is the support on which and from which labour is exerted. Fertility is not a toil the earth performs, it is a natural condition that labour exploits.

This chapter aims to dispel one error: confusing the condition of a production with its agent. The land does nothing; it allows something to be done: a field, a forest, a house.

The consequence is immediate, and decisive. The owner, as owner, produces nothing. He is neither the father (labour) nor the mother (the land) of Petty’s metaphor2: he is a third party, who holds the title over the mother. If he drains, builds, improves, irrigates, that part is labour and capital, which must be credited to him as such; but it is not rent. Rent properly so called is what he draws from the bare title, after deducting every improvement. This dividing line (what pays for a real production on one side, what pays for mere holding on the other) is no refinement: it is the discipline that will govern the whole empirical measurement of part III.

1.2 Conditioning: the necessity of a place

What, then, does conditioning mean? All human activity is situated. To dwell, to produce, to store, simply to stand upright, one must occupy a portion of space. The body has an extension, labour has a place, the commodity has a warehouse.

Now, useful space is finite and, for the most part, not reproducible. One does not manufacture an additional centre for a city; one does not multiply at will proximity, access, situation. Sedentary life, as soon as a population settles and concentrates, makes situated land a scarce and disputed resource, and it is from this scarcity, not from any productive virtue, that the power of land is born. This power is not productive but positional: it is the power of the gate over those who must pass through. To hold the land necessary to an activity is not to contribute to that activity: it is to command the bottleneck through which it must pass.

What makes the thing possible is that nobody produced the land: its existence is a natural fact and its scarcity a social fact, the product of no one.3 And yet it is this condition that no one made which is appropriated and whose access is monetised: the toll is rooted in the private appropriation of a condition that nobody produced.

1.3 Rent: the toll of conditioning

Rent can henceforth be defined simply: it is the toll that the holder of the condition levies in order to grant access to it. Let us say at once what it is not. It is not the payment for a productive service, for the land renders none. It is not the yield of an improvement — buildings, drainage, equipment —, for that belongs to capital and interest, which must be kept apart. Nor is it the “marginal productivity” of a “land” factor, a naturalisation that standard economics would perform later and that will be refuted in its place.

Classical economics itself, which had no interest in admitting it, glimpsed this without understanding it. Ricardo noted that rent does not enter into the cost of production but results from it: “corn is not high because a rent is paid, but a rent is paid because corn is high”; and that the landlords could give up the whole of their rent without the price of corn falling.4 What the classics thus record in spite of themselves, without knowing how to say it, is that rent adds nothing to the product but is levied upon it — a tribute, the word that Lenin, for his part, uses without reserve.5

Hence a rule that will govern all the rest. In every sum paid to gain access to a space, one must separate what pays for a real production — the building, the upkeep, the labour, the capital engaged — from what pays only for the condition, the bare fact of being admitted: the first part is a price, the second a rent. To confuse the two is to pass a toll off as a service: that is precisely the operation the statistical nomenclatures perform, and that part II will undo.

1.4 A definition prior to capitalism

This definition presupposes nothing of capitalism. It mobilises neither the market, nor wage labour, nor the law of value; it holds wherever sedentary life makes situated space a scarce resource. The temple of Uruk assigning its fields, the feudal lord levying his dues, today’s landlord collecting his rent: three holders of a condition, three tolls. What distinguishes them is the form of the levy — redistributive levy, status-based due, market price; and this typology of forms will be the subject of the next chapter. What does not change is the mechanism: a toll on the conditioning of access.

The price-form is merely the latest comer, and the most complete: it makes the tribute transferable, maximisable, universal, in the same way that shareholding renders this service to capital, though in a less accomplished form if one sets aside real-estate securities. But it did not invent the tribute; it generalised a phenomenon of long history. It is in this precise sense that this book holds rent to be the oldest political problem of sedentary societies: it is as old as the fact of having to be somewhere. Marx will analyse its accomplished capitalist form; Lefebvre and Harvey will follow its transposition to urban space, where fertility becomes location6; and from it we shall draw, in chapter 5, the class subjects — the user and the rentier. But the core is laid down here, deliberately before them.

Taking stock

Land does not produce, it conditions; rent is the toll levied on this conditioning. The rentier is the third party that Petty’s metaphor passes over in silence: neither the father, who works, nor the mother, who bears7, but the one who holds the title over the mother and makes the father pay for permission to approach her, thus levying, without adding anything to the child, upon its very conception.

The historical forms of the levy (chapter 2), its analysis in its accomplished capitalist figure (chapter 3), its transposition to built space (chapter 4), the classes it sets against each other (chapter 5): all of this unfolds from a single distinction, which must never again be lost from sight — that between production and toll. Never count the tribute as a production: that is the instruction this chapter bequeaths to the empirical measurement.
