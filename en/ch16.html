Part Four — Allocating land otherwise · Chapter 16

16 The transition: the decree or the scale

Violence is the midwife of every old society that carries a new one in its womb.

Karl Marx, Capital, Volume I, chap. xxxi (Marx 1867).

The regime is now founded: the two preceding chapters have laid down its form (the communalised land, the inalienable title of use) and its institution (the scale of need, the commissions drawn by lot, the upkeep contribution). But a regime founded on paper is not thereby instituted, and between the one and the other lies the passage: how does one go from the order of price to the order of use? That is the question of the transition, and it is the hardest, because it is no longer a question of doctrine but of force.

The introduction named it in advance: never do the dominant yield the power they hold over the land without being compelled to. To abolish rent is not to amend a policy; it is to take from a class the income it draws from the holding of space, and no class lends itself to that with good grace. This chapter examines two roads to that end — the revolutionary road, by decree; the reformist road, in the Viennese manner, by capping rents — and it shows that both run up against the same wall: the legal armature with which the proprietary order has surrounded itself, and, behind it, the force that defends it. We argue here, openly, a strategy; we predict nothing, we project, from the precedents this book has gathered, what each road would have to face.

16.1 The wall

Before examining the two roads, the obstacle must be measured: lucrative property is not guarded by habit alone; it is fortified, in law, on three storeys. At the bottom, the constitutional block: the Declaration of 1789 ranks property among the “inviolable and sacred” rights, and the Constitutional Council has made it a right of constitutional rank, of which no deprivation is admitted without a “fair and prior” indemnity — so it ruled on the nationalisations of 1982.1 Above it, the conventional storey: the first Protocol to the European Convention on Human Rights guarantees “the peaceful enjoyment of possessions”, and the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union enshrines property in its article 17. At the top, the Union treaties: the free movement of capital, which forbids in principle stopping money at the border.

And the case law is not neutral: it has already ruled against taking housing out of the market. The Strasbourg Court condemned, as a violation of the landlord’s right, the protected rent Yugoslavia had instituted (this book noted it in chapter 12) and, likewise, Polish rent control.2 But — and the nuance decides everything — the same Court upheld the Austrian statutory rent reduction, as a proportionate regulation of use.3 The line the Court draws is, word for word, the one this whole book has drawn: a rent that covers the cost is tolerated; a rent that falls below the cost, and confiscates, is censured. The law itself polices the threshold between the cost and the tribute — we shall see that it is on this wire that the second road is played out.

Behind the law, finally, material force. Capital flight, which within days punishes any government that worries property; the bond market, which raises the cost of debt for the recalcitrant State; the Union’s instruments of coercion — infringement procedures, the article 7 mechanism, and the monetary weapon, the Central Bank cutting off liquidity, as Greece experienced in 2015; and the natural disposition of the dominant to resort to open violence when all else has failed. The transition must therefore be situated within a balance of forces of which the law is only the juridical transposition. The two roads differ in the way they confront it.

16.2 The revolutionary road: the decree

The first road socialises at a stroke, by decree: it communalises the land, converts occupation into titles of use, abolishes lucrative property — the programme of the two preceding chapters, promulgated at one go. It is the gesture of the Soviet Union in 1918, of Cuba in 1960, of Libya in 1978; but rid of what undid them. No kommunalka: the decree socialises the title, not the sharing of dwellings; the home is inviolable from the first day, and no one has a stranger assigned within his walls. Upkeep is financed from the outset by the contribution, allocation handed to the commissions drawn by lot: the decree thus abolishes nothing but the rent, and guarantees use from the outset.

But the conflict is frontal, and immediate. With the European legal order first. Not that socialising is forbidden in itself: the judges admit nationalisation in the general interest (the Strasbourg Court as well as the Constitutional Council), but at a price, the indemnity, fair and prior.4 What the decree refuses is that price: to abolish lucrative property without buying it out is deprivation without compensation, frontally censured by Protocol 1 and by article 17 of the Charter — the Strasbourg Court would condemn, and the Union would retaliate. The road of the decree therefore cannot be travelled so long as one remains bound by those treaties: it presupposes denouncing the Convention and breaking with the Union. The introduction had sensed it — “constitutional revision would not suffice; it would have to be carried to the conventional level as well”. The consequence is clear-cut: one does not socialise the land while keeping the treaties that sanctify the tribute, and the decree is therefore, in itself, a rupture with the European legal order.

With the capitalist world next. Capital flight is instantaneous and massive: holders move their assets as soon as the decree becomes foreseeable. The currency falls; the State must institute exchange controls (in violation of the free movement of capital) and take the banks, for the very reason that makes it take the land: a half-socialisation that leaves finance free is defeated by finance. Then come trade retaliations, perhaps the embargo — Cuba bore that burden for sixty years. The road leans towards autarky, or towards alliance with whatever, in the world, is not the market.

And at home, counter-revolution. The dominant do not yield: 1934 in Vienna, 1973 in Chile, 1871 in Paris say it well enough. The decree must be defended, and that is the precise meaning, stripped of its legends, of what the tradition calls the dictatorship of the proletariat: not a tyranny, but the defence of the new order against those who would overthrow it by force.

What, then, would a socialisation of housing such as the preceding chapter described produce? A decisive advantage, first: the privatisation that undid the Soviet Union, Yugoslavia and Cuba is here structurally impossible — the inalienable title of use cannot be sold —, so that the decree is the only road that closes the window of reflux from the outset. A heavy difficulty, next, that of construction: the private capital of the building trade flees; the communes, the State, the cooperatives must take its place at once — Viennese municipal construction carried to the scale of a country, the Soviet khrushchevki brought out of the ground by the million — on pain of finding again the chronic shortage that was the plague of the USSR. The danger of the decree is not forced sharing, which the form forbids; it is the void of construction and upkeep, if the State does not fill at once the place of the capital that has fled.

But that very void, the nature of the object makes it passable, and this is the strongest argument of the road by decree: unlike capital, which expatriates itself, the land cannot flee, and the decree seizes precisely what cannot be moved — one does not carry off a hectare to Luxembourg. What flees is not the land, it is the financial claim on the land; and the decree, by socialising the immovable in the literal sense (that which does not move), captures the one form of wealth the strike of capital cannot withdraw.

The road of the decree is thus swift, irreversible (no reflux possible), and it seizes the inexpatriable; but it means open rupture with the European order and the capitalist world, capital flight, the embargo, counter-revolution, and the necessity of defending by force what has been instituted by law. It is the road the thesis of the introduction designated.

16.3 The reformist road: the Viennese scale

The second road does not decree abolition: it obtains it by a detour. A transitional social-democratic government caps rents (the scale) and lets the mechanism established in chapter 4 do its work: the price of land is nothing but capitalised rent. Once the rent is capped, the expected rent vanishes; with it evaporates the “capital” that the price of land was; the land is no longer worth anything, and the collectivity acquires it for next to nothing, then builds and socialises. It is the method of Vienna, which chapter 12 described: the rent freeze — a conservative federal law! — collapsed the value of the plots, and the city bought a quarter of Greater Vienna at a seventh or a tenth of pre-war prices. The reformist road does not expropriate; it regulates the rent (a recognised power) and lets the value dissolve of itself, before acquiring at a knock-down price the land thus depreciated.

This road therefore works within the proprietary order to hollow it out — no expropriation without compensation, “only” a regulation of rent. That is why it can pass the legal filter — but up to a point, and that point is exactly the threshold the law polices. A cap that covers the cost is upheld. A cap below the cost, confiscatory, is censured. The reformist cap must therefore be set at the cost of the building — covering the owner’s real charges, upkeep, management, but suppressing the rent, the income of the land. And that is, word for word, the contribution of chapter 15: rent at cost. The legal survival of the reformist road depends on its cap being the rent-at-cost the book wants in any case; it can plead, before the Constitutional Council and before Strasbourg, that it regulates (the cost) and does not deprive (rent being not a protected asset, but a tribute). And to this plea the law gives more purchase than one would think.

For this threshold (the cost) is precisely the target the book aims at. A cap that collapses the rent without falling below the cost is upheld: the Strasbourg Court said so of the Austrian reduction, the Constitutional Council of the French rent-control scheme.5 Conventional law thus traces, unwittingly, the target of the reform: abolish rent down to the cost, not below. There remains a thin margin, and this is the real tightrope: Strasbourg requires not the bare cost, but the cost plus a “decent profit” — a residue of rent it guarantees the landlord.6 So as not to restore it by way of the indemnity, the collectivity must buy back the residual title at its collapsed value (at cost), once the speculative value is extinguished: a partial indemnity, which the judges admit (James, Lithgow), provided it is not derisory. The wall of the reformist road is therefore not, at bottom, a legal one — the law leaves it the narrow passage of the cost; it lies elsewhere.

And the Union, and the market. The franc, the bond market, capital flight — that is the precedent of 1981-1983: a government of the left, having frightened capital, faces the flight, the currency crisis, the European monetary constraint, and the “turn to austerity”, the forced retreat.7 Greece in 2015 showed its crudest form: the European Central Bank cuts off liquidity, and the government of the left capitulates within days.8 The reformist road is exposed to the monetary weapon precisely because it remains within the order that wields it.

There remains, finally, the most mortal peril, the reflux: if Vienna succeeded, it is not for having capped, it is for having run — for having converted the collapsed value into socialised property, buying the land and building, before the reflux could organise itself. A government that merely caps without acquiring leaves the value on the ground but the title private; and the next right-wing government raises the caps, the value rebounds, the dominant recover everything: the cap without acquisition will have been nothing but a loan granted to the future counter-reform. And Vienna’s very race did not save its political project: the artillery came in 1934. The institution survived (chapter 12 said so), but only because the land had already been bought and built upon. The lesson is exact: the reformist road is worth anything only if it transforms the collapsed value into socialised property faster than the reflux forms — and, even then, it does not escape the artillery; it only ensures that what the artillery cannot take back, the socialised stock already built, will outlive it.

This road is no relic of the 1920s: at the moment this book is being completed, two metropolises of the capitalist core are replaying it before our eyes. In New York, a mayor elected in the autumn of 2025 on the watchword of the rent freeze (“freeze the rent”) holds the organ that sets them: the rent board, whose nine members he appoints, voted in June 2026 the outright freeze (for one year and, a thing without precedent, for two) of the rent of about one million “stabilised” dwellings, more than four rental dwellings in ten in the city.9 And the mechanism Vienna had revealed can be observed there in real time: since a 2019 law hardened stabilisation, the value of the buildings concerned has melted by about 40 % — the “capital” evaporates as soon as the rent ceases to grow.10 In Berlin, the other half of the road: the voters approved as early as 2021, by 59 %, the socialisation of the stocks of the big landlords (some 240 000 dwellings), a mandate left a dead letter; the rent cap attempted by the city had, for its part, been annulled by the Federal Constitutional Court, for want of competence of the Land. But socialisation falls, for its part, under the Basic Law (article 15), within the Land’s competence; it is on its implementation that the campaign for the Berlin election of autumn 2026 turns, a second referendum, binding this time, being in preparation.11 The cap on one side, socialisation on the other: each of the two cities holds one half of the Viennese race — New York freezes rents without acquiring the stock, Berlin wants to acquire the stock without having been able to freeze rents —, and nothing yet says that either will manage to run it whole.

The reformist road is thus gradual, legally finer, less violent at first; but it is a race against the reflux and the legal counter-attack, exposed to the monetary weapon, and one which, pushed to its end — socialising for good — meets the same wall as the decree: when the dominant see where the cap leads, they resist with the same force. Either it stops halfway and is defeated — Mitterrand, Greece; or it goes all the way and the artillery comes — Vienna: that is why the Viennese method must be understood as a tactic of the transition, not as a way out that would dispense with it.

16.4 The counter-proof: Athens and Lisbon

The reformist road rests entirely on one mechanism — the collapse of land value — which one might think exceptional, almost nowhere to be found outside the Viennese laboratory. It is not. The crises of capital produce it by themselves, periodically; and the decade of the 2010s has just administered, on two European capitals, the lesson of what happens when the opportunity is not seized: the Viennese window opened wide there, and the public authorities did not rush into it.

In Athens, the crisis did what the Viennese freeze had done. Between the third quarter of 2008 and the third quarter of 2017, the Bank of Greece’s index of apartment prices loses 42.4 %.12 A quarter of the domestic product had vanished, and the minimum wage had been cut by 22 % by the second memorandum (Lapavitsas 2019). At 2017 prices, the window was open: the land of Athens cost half what it had in 2008, in a city where the need to inhabit, for its part, had not diminished. But the memoranda that had organised this deflation commanded the opposite of public acquisition: they enjoined selling. The privatisation fund demanded by the creditors thus ceded, in 2016, the 620 hectares of the former Hellinikon airport — one of the largest building plots in Europe, whose sale the troika had been demanding since 2010 — to a private group, for 915 million euros, “three times less than its real value” according to the estimates reported in the press (Garcia 2018).

Who, then, bought the trough? Not the inhabitants, whose incomes had just been cut down, but the most liquid capital — and the State itself invited it in. From 2013, a “golden visa” offers a European residence permit against 250 000 euros of property; nearly 80 000 permits are in force today, and the government, by since raising the threshold to 800 000 euros in tight-market zones “to combat the housing crisis”, has itself measured the effect it ascribes to it (Ministère grec des Migrations et de l’Asile 2025). The banks’ doubtful claims, backed by the dwellings of insolvent households, were securitised and entrusted to four debt-collection companies, with foreign-fund capital, which manage nine tenths of them — of the order of 70 billion euros (Fonds monétaire international 2022); by September 2020, more than 55 000 dwellings had gone, or were scheduled to go, to electronic auction (Sideris 2020). Tourist lets did the rest, to the point that the State had to freeze, in 2025, any new registration in the central districts of Athens. The result holds in two curves that cross: since the trough, the price index has regained 90 %, the 2008 peak has been exceeded since the end of 2024 — while real wages remain below their 2010 level. Greece now displays the highest housing cost overburden rate in the Union: 28.9 % of households devote more than 40 % of their income to housing, against 18.1 % before the crisis (Eurostat 2025a). The collapse did not abolish rent; it made it change hands and scale — from the Athenian landlord to the global fund.

In Lisbon, the restoration was not even left to chance: it was written down. The memorandum the troika had Portugal sign on 17 May 2011, in return for its loan, contains an entire section on the housing market, which commands the exit from rent control and swift eviction.13 The law of 2012 executes the order — unfreezing of old leases, a national eviction desk (République portugaise 2012) — while, as in Athens, the State invites global capital in: a golden visa (12 718 residence permits sold in ten years, 6.45 billion euros of property (Serviço de Estrangeiros e Fronteiras 2023)), a derogatory tax regime for foreign rentiers, a simplified registration of tourist lets. The result exceeds that of Athens: the national price index gains 135.7 % between 2015 and 2024 (Eurostat 2025b), and the Portuguese statistical institute records a figure that amounts to a demonstration: in Greater Lisbon, the buyer domiciled abroad pays 65 % more than the resident (Instituto Nacional de Estatística 2025). The price of Lisbon’s land is no longer set by the use of those who live there, but by the world demand for placement — locational value in its pure state, detached from the last link with need. And when, in 2023, a government tried to close the valve — the end of the property golden visa, a freeze on tourist lets, the compulsory letting of vacant dwellings —, the law was unpicked the very next year by the majority that succeeded it (République portugaise 2023): the reflux, once again, and without even a socialised stock having been built in the meantime.

From this counter-proof, the transition must retain three things. First, that windows will not be lacking: the collapse of land values is no artifice of a scale, it is a periodic product of the crises of capital, and it will recur. Next, that the collapsed value has no camp: the opportunity Vienna seized for its inhabitants, Athens and Lisbon left to the most liquid capital, and the tribute reconstituted itself, more intense and more concentrated than before the crisis — the collectivity that does not buy the trough lets it, in effect, be bought by others. Finally, that the wall described at the opening of this chapter does not content itself with defending, but attacks: the same order that cut off Athens’ liquidity in the summer of 2015 had written down in Lisbon, four years earlier and in black and white, the dismantling of rent control. Facing it, abstention is not a fallback position: it is one of the two outcomes, the one in which the land changes hands upwards. It is armed with this counter-proof that the two roads must, finally, be compared.

The same threshold

The two roads differ in tempo and in form, not in the wall they meet. And that wall, as we have seen, is not legal in the first instance. The law leaves a passage: it admits socialisation provided it compensates, and it bounds the cap at the cost — which is the very target of the reform. The decree, refusing to pay, chooses rupture; the scale, because it pays in collapsed currency, manages to pass. What stops the transition is therefore not the paper armour, but the force it covers: capital flight, the monetary weapon, the reflux, and the artillery when all else has failed. The difference between the two roads is not between legality and illegality, nor between peace and violence: it is between the avowed rupture (the decree, which refuses the price and the European order at one stroke) and the deferred rupture (the scale, which pays, temporises, and confronts force only at the moment when force judges, as in 1934, that the land is slipping from its grasp). The treaties that make lucrative property an “inviolable and sacred” right are, in the last analysis, only the juridical form of that force: it can be circumvented through the law, it cannot be avoided in fact.

The thesis of the introduction is thereby confirmed, and made more precise: through the ballot box or not, in every case in a revolutionary way, for never do the dominant yield their power without resorting to violence. The reformist road is not an alternative to revolution: it is one of its tactics — or a reformism that stops halfway and is overturned. The decisive variable is not the method, but the balance of forces: the transition will hold if it knows how to defend itself against the reflux and the counter-revolution, and to seize, then keep, the only wealth that cannot flee — the land.

It is here that this book stops, as it must. It has shown that rent is contingent (Parts I and II), it has measured it (Part III), it has shown how to abolish it and proposed how to allocate the land otherwise (Part IV), and it has named the threshold of force every transition must cross. What it cannot do is cross it: that is not the work of theory, but of a movement, of a balance of forces that analysis illuminates without creating it.

Let us remember, to end, that with which this book opened: nine hundred and twenty-nine people dead “without a home”, and a mother evicted so that a title might yield its income again. The regime we have described would have spared them — not out of charity, but by abolishing the tribute that killed them. That it be instituted is no longer a question of theory; it is a question of force. A balance of force is built, patiently, like houses. That is the work of the citizens, the political organisations, the trade unions and the associations already labouring at it.

Housing to the inhabitant, the workshop to the worker, the land to the peasant.
