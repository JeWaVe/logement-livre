Part One — A theory of rent and space · Chapter 3

3 Rent in Marx: the analysis of the accomplished form

Landed property presupposes that certain persons enjoy a monopoly over determinate portions of the earth’s surface, with the privilege of disposing of them as they will, to the exclusion of all others.

Karl Marx, Capital, Book III, chap. xxxvii (Marx 1894).

The preceding chapters dealt with the origin and the historical forms of the land toll: rent is a tribute levied on the conditioning of access, and its price-form is late and contingent. Marx enters this book not as a theorist of origins (that role belongs to the long span of history, chapters 1 and 2), but as the analyst of the most developed form: capitalist ground rent, the accomplished figure of the toll, dissected in Book III of Capital, part VI. This chapter follows him through the forms he distinguishes — differential rent (I and II), absolute rent, monopoly rent — in order to extract what the thesis of this book retains: under capitalism, rent becomes a pure income of title, freely transferable, maximisable, and even capitalisable into a “price of land”.

We proceed here as analysts, not as judges: the political conclusion (the abolition of rent) belongs to Part IV. From the outset we draw the discipline that Part III will have to observe: never confuse rent properly so called, the tribute of title, with the interest on capital actually incorporated into the soil.

3.1 The framework: a monopoly, a triad, a tribute

Rent, in Marx, presupposes a monopoly. Not a market monopoly in the ordinary sense, but the juridical monopoly by which certain persons hold a portion of the globe and can exclude all others from it: landed property. The whole analysis then consists in determining “what the economic value of this monopoly is in capitalist production”.1 Rent is the form in which this monopoly realises itself economically; and with it the three classes of modern society reappear (wage labourers, industrial capitalists, landed proprietors): the capitalist farmer pays the proprietor, in order to be able to apply his capital to the soil, a sum of money fixed by contract, and this sum is ground rent.2

What this framework establishes, and what governs everything else, is that rent is not the price of a productive service, but a tribute. Where chapter 1 posited it as a matter of principle (land does not produce, it conditions), Marx says it in the very terms of the capitalist relation: the land “is now for the landed proprietor nothing but the source of a tribute which his monopoly allows him to levy on the farmer, the agricultural capitalist”.3

From this follows a distinction that governs the empirical measurement of Part III. Capital may be incorporated into the soil — drainage, levelling, irrigation, buildings; this capital belongs to fixed capital, and “the interest it yields may form part of the rent that the farmer pays to the landed proprietor, but it does not constitute ground rent properly so called, which is paid for the use of the soil, whether cultivated or not”.4

3.2 Differential rent (I and II): Ricardo turned around

The first form is the one that classical economics had already glimpsed. Differential rent arises from the fact that equal capitals, applied to unequal lands, yield unequal products; the excess, a surplus profit, is transformed into rent for the benefit of the proprietor. Two causes govern it: “fertility […]; situation”.5 The price that regulates the market is that of the worst land under cultivation;6 rent, therefore, does not enter into the price: it results from it. Marx formulates this through the analogy of the waterfall, a monopolisable natural force: this rent “is a result and not a factor of the general cost of production”; the surplus profit falls to its holder “not as capitalist, but as proprietor […] the possession of a natural force, limited, monopolisable and independent of capital”.7

Marx thus takes over Ricardo’s differential mechanism, but he turns it around in two respects. Ricardo defined rent as the payment for “the original and indestructible powers of the soil”, a naturalised fertility, and admitted no rent other than differential rent.8 Marx establishes on the contrary that fertility is a natural condition that labour exploits, not a productive virtue of the soil, mocking “the theorists […] who […] maintain that here value is determined not by labour but by nature”;9 and he denies that the worst land is free of rent: that is the subject of the next section.

Before that, he complicates differential rent with a second form. Differential rent I results from equal capitals applied to different lands; differential rent II, from capitals applied successively to the same land.10 Both say the same thing: the more capital is applied to the land, “the greater becomes the tribute that society pays in the form of surplus profits to the landed proprietors”.11

3.3 Absolute rent: the point where Marx pushes furthest — and is most exposed

Differential rent leaves the worst land without rent; Ricardo stopped there. The step proper to Marx — the one that chapter 1 reserved for him — is absolute rent: even the worst land pays. “The rent of the worst land therefore does not have its source in a difference of fertility”.12 On that land, rent is not the effect of the price but its cause: “it is landed property itself that provokes this increase in price and creates this rent”.13 Property acts as a toll that bars access until it has been paid: “Landed property therefore acts as a barrier that prevents […] any advance of capital on lands not yet cultivated […] if landed property did not claim its tribute”.14

Absolute rent would thus be the pure case of the toll: a tribute levied on the mere holding of the condition, “apart from any difference in fertility” to take up the formula of chapter 1, by a proprietor who neither produces nor improves anything. One understands the temptation to make it the keystone of the thesis; it is nonetheless the most exposed point of the whole construction, and it is better to put it to the test than to lean on it: the thesis, as we shall see, owes it nothing essential.

The stumbling block is its derivation: Marx grounds it in the organic composition of agricultural capital, lower according to him than the social average, and he himself marked its limit — absolute rent would vanish if that composition caught up with the average.15 Now mechanisation has ruled against him: since the tractor and the harvester replaced the team and the day labourer, capital per agricultural worker has caught up with the average of enterprises.16 The premise of the derivation is therefore, for developed agriculture, empirically obsolete.

A still more radical critique targets not the premise but the category: as soon as one ceases to take the quantity of capital advanced by the farmer as given, and derives it instead from his calculation, absolute rent vanishes at equilibrium, on the worst land as on the best.17 Absolute rent then appears as a category of transition, tied (as Marx admitted) to a stage at which quasi-feudal impediments still weighed on the allocation of capital.

On this terrain, Marx had a methodological defender: Lenin, who dismantles the “law of diminishing returns” which the revisionist “critics” had made the cornerstone of their refutation — this “law” holds only with unchanged technique, and it is precisely Marx who “freed the theory of differential rent from any link with the famous law of diminishing returns”.18 And Lenin draws the consequence that this book, for its part, reserves for Part IV: absolute rent being the tribute of the monopoly of private property in land, not of its exploitation, the nationalisation of the land would abolish it.19

There is, however, no cause for alarm: the thesis of this book has never rested on absolute rent as a category — Lenin, already, indicated the reason by lodging the tribute in property, not in the composition of capital. What withstands all these objections — and what Marx, Lenin, Harvey and the most severe of his critics equally concede — is the only point the book needs: no land is ceded for nothing so long as private property in land subsists; the proprietor, who produces nothing, captures its surplus profit as a tribute. Rent, whether one calls it absolute, differential or monopoly rent, remains that toll.20 The focus of the conflict has, in any case, shifted to urban land (as we shall see in the next chapter), where the question of organic composition no longer even arises.21

3.4 The accomplished form: rent becomes title, then capital

Why does Marx hold the capitalist form to be the accomplished form? Because it is the terminus of a genesis: labour rent (the corvée, where “unpaid surplus labour takes […] the form of rent”), then rent in kind, then money rent, a “form of dissolution” that calls forth the capitalist farmer.22 With the capitalist farmer, the relation tips over: “It is no longer the land, it is capital that holds agricultural labour under its yoke”; profit becomes the normal form of surplus value, and rent “is now no more than a remnant of the surplus value that the capitalist farmer extracts by means of his capital from the agricultural worker”.23

It is this tipping over that gives capitalist rent its decisive trait, and that makes it the accomplished form of the toll: it becomes a pure income of title. Three properties distinguish it from the status-based dues of chapter 2. It is transferable: it is sold with the land. It is maximisable: raised to what the market will bear, not fixed by custom. It is, finally, capitalisable: the “price of land” is nothing other than capitalised rent, a price without value, since no labour is materialised in the land.24 The title thus turns into a financial asset, valued by the flow of tribute it promises. As for monopoly rent properly so called (the rent that arises from a monopoly price), its decisive contemporary figure is urban; we shall meet it again, with real-estate rent, in chapters 4 and 5.

Summary

Marx’s analysis confirms the thesis of the book. Rent, in all its forms, is a tribute, a deduction: the landed proprietor produces nothing (chapter 1), he captures a surplus profit that title confers upon him. Marx had pursued this tribute right down to its limiting case — absolute rent, levied “apart from any difference in fertility” on the worst land; this limiting case, as we have seen, is contested and probably obsolete, but it matters little, for the tribute survives in the forms that remain and in the simple fact that no land is free so long as property endures. The differential rent of situation, in particular, is the agricultural germ of what chapter 4 will carry over to built space, where location becomes the mainspring of rent.

And the capitalist form is indeed the accomplished form: rent there becomes an income of title, transferable, maximisable, capitalisable — the “price of land” as capitalised rent. There lies the economic motor of the regime of the price (chapter 2) and the germ of the financialisation that Part III will measure. From this chapter, empirical measurement inherits a non-negotiable discipline: to separate rent properly so called — the tribute of title — from the interest on capital actually incorporated into the soil (chapter xxxvii), on pain of counting the toll as a production — the error that chapter 1 proscribed, and the trap that Part II will defuse in the nomenclatures.

A methodological reservation, finally: absolute rent, as we have shown, is the most exposed link — a premise empirically obsolete, a category contested to the point of annulment ((Basu 2022)). The book does not depend on it; it retains only the robust core, on which Marx and his critics agree: no land is free so long as property endures, and the proprietor, who produces nothing, captures its tribute. The analysis stops here; whether this tribute ought to be abolished, and how, is a normative question, and that is the business of Part IV. It remains to take the step that Marx sketched and that Lefebvre and Harvey systematised: from agricultural rent to the rent of built space. That is the subject of the next chapter.
